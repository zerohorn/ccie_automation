Mental snapshot

  • Dial-out means the device is the client. It opens the connection to a collector you name, so the config lives on the device and survives reboots.
  • On IOS XE this is a configured subscription: telemetry ietf subscription N, stream yang-push, encoding encode-kvgpb, transport gRPC (grpc-tcp or grpc-tls).
  • On NX-OS it is the three-part model: destination-group (where), sensor-group (what), subscription (glue plus cadence).
  • Units differ and the exam will punish you for it: IOS XE periodic is in centiseconds, NX-OS sample-interval is in milliseconds.
  • Receivers cannot be edited in place on IOS XE. Delete and recreate. Only the first receiver of a subscription is used.
  • Verification ladder: subscription Valid only proves the config parsed. Receiver state and bytes arriving at the collector prove delivery.
  • Compared with yesterday's dial-in units: no client session to keep alive, and nothing disappears on a switchover.

Where this fits

Fundamentals to exam depth

L1Concept

You already know SNMP polling and traps. Model-driven telemetry replaces both with a publisher/subscriber model over structured data: you describe what (a YANG path), how often (period or on-change) and where (receiver), and the device pushes. The only question dial-out answers is who opens the TCP session.

In dial-in the collector connects to the device (gNMI on a gRPC server, or NETCONF) and the subscription dies with that session. In dial-out the device holds the subscription in its running configuration and connects out. RFC 8639 calls the second kind configured subscriptions and says the publisher uses call-home style connection setup when it has something to send. The practical consequences are what the design question tests:

  • Collectors behind NAT or a firewall are no problem, because only the device needs a route to the collector.
  • Scale is easier to reason about. The device pushes to one or a few collectors and you do not maintain thousands of inbound sessions.
  • Subscriptions persist across reboots and survive a stack or HA switchover, because they are configuration.
  • The cost: the receiver list is configuration, so changing a collector means changing device config, which is exactly the job a pipeline or Ansible play should do.

L2Mechanics

On IOS XE the telemetry publisher is the pubd process inside the DMI (data management interface) stack. That is why netconf-yang must be configured and running before a configured subscription does anything, even though you are not using NETCONF as the transport. The guide also lists the NETCONF notification capability as a prerequisite. Check the process list when nothing works.

A configured subscription has five parts. The stream is yang-push, which reads from the configuration and operational datastores. The filter is an XPath into a YANG module, written with the module prefix, for example /memory-ios-xe-oper:memory-statistics/memory-statistic. The update policy is periodic (a number in centiseconds, minimum 100, so one second) or on-change. The encoding must be encode-kvgpb for gRPC. The receiver is an address, a port and a protocol. Two optional lines control the path out: source-vrf and source-address.

The gRPC session itself is HTTP/2 over TCP. The device acts as the gRPC client and streams messages to a service the collector implements. The payload is key-value Google Protocol Buffers, which flattens the YANG tree into name/value pairs with timestamps. A collector that only speaks JSON will not decode it. (The exact service and message names are defined by Cisco's dial-out proto file; I did not fetch that file, so treat names as ⚠ unverified.)

RFC 8639 receiver states

The standard defines four receiver states for a configured subscription. IOS XE shows its own state names in show output (the guide mentions connecting and connected values), so map them rather than expect identical words.

L3Exam detail

The blueprint verb is design, so expect task shapes like: given a collector address and a requirement (cadence, security, what to measure), pick the direction and transport, then produce a working configured subscription and prove delivery. Pay attention to these traps.

Additional limits from the IOS XE guide: no subtree filters, no authorization check on the data sent, no automatic hierarchy selection for on-change, no subscription QoS, and a platform-dependent ceiling of about 100 subscriptions. A configured subscription cannot be deleted by a delete-subscription RPC. Remove the configuration. If you build the subscription through NETCONF instead of the CLI, the model is Cisco-IOS-XE-mdt-cfg and the list is mdt-subscription, as in the Anatomy section.

On TLS: the 17.5 guide says TLS for gRPC dial-out arrived in 17.1.1 and shows the receiver line with a profile argument. The gNOI chapter in the same book explains how certificates and trustpoints are installed or rotated by RPC, and says those trustpoints persist across switchovers. The guide's own examples spell the protocol both grpc-tls and grpc_tls. Use ? on the device to see which the parser takes (⚠ unverified). Whether the device requires the collector to present a certificate, a client certificate in return, or both is not settled by the pages I fetched. Test it (⚠).

Design heuristic. Choose dial-out when the collector sits behind NAT, when you want subscriptions to survive HA events without a client babysitting them, or when config should be reviewable in Git. Choose dial-in when many different consumers need ad hoc, short-lived queries.

Anatomy

Snippets pinned to the 17.5 guide. They show structure, not your drill answer.

IOS XE, periodic, plain gRPC (17.5 guide example)

telemetry ietf subscription 101
 encoding encode-kvgpb
 filter xpath /memory-ios-xe-oper:memory-statistics/memory-statistic
 stream yang-push
 update-policy periodic 6000          ! 6000 cs = 60 s
 source-vrf Mgmt-intf                 ! VRF that reaches the collector
 receiver ip address 10.28.35.45 57555 protocol grpc-tcp

IOS XE, on-change with TLS (17.5 guide example)

telemetry ietf subscription 8
 stream yang-push
 filter xpath /iosxe-oper:ios-oper-db/hwidb-table
 update-policy on-change
 encoding encode-kvgpb
 receiver ip address 10.22.22.45 45000 protocol grpc_tls profile secure_profile

Same thing as NETCONF edit-config (17.5 guide)

<mdt-config-data xmlns="http://cisco.com/ns/yang/Cisco-IOS-XE-mdt-cfg">
  <mdt-subscription>
    <subscription-id>200</subscription-id>
    <base>
      <stream>yang-push</stream>
      <encoding>encode-kvgpb</encoding>
      <period>6000</period>
      <xpath>/memory-ios-xe-oper:memory-statistics/memory-statistic</xpath>
    </base>
    <mdt-receivers>
      <address>10.22.23.48</address>
      <port>57555</port>
      <protocol>grpc-tcp</protocol>
    </mdt-receivers>
  </mdt-subscription>
</mdt-config-data>

The wrapper (rpc, edit-config, target running) is omitted here. Note period carries the same centisecond value as the CLI.

NX-OS dial-out (destination syntax from the 9.3(x) chapter; the rest follows the newer 10.6(4) doc, ⚠ for 9.3(8))

Speed notes

  • On the device, type telemetry ietf subscription 1 then ? at each level. The parser lists streams, encodings and receiver protocols, which also settles the grpc-tls spelling.
  • show telemetry ietf subscription all then ... 101 detail then the receiver view for connection state. The lab README I read uses show telemetry ietf subscription 201 receiver (⚠ confirm syntax on 17.16).
  • show running-config | section telemetry is faster than reading detail when you suspect typo or unit errors.
  • To see the model offline: pyang -f tree against the Cisco-IOS-XE-mdt-cfg module, if the YANG files are on the workstation (⚠ unverified location). Otherwise pull the module from the device with a NETCONF get-schema.
  • Collector side on Ubuntu: ss -ltn to confirm the listener, tcpdump -ni any tcp port 57555 to see who connects and from which source address. If the source IP is not the one you intended, fix source-address first.
  • Unit check before every submit: IOS XE centiseconds, NX-OS milliseconds. 60 seconds is 6000 versus 60000.

Micro-drill (about 10 min)

Target: Cat8000V (IOS XE, lab runs 17.16). IOSv and IOSvL2 do not support this. Collector: your Ubuntu workstation.

Requirement. Send memory statistics every 30 seconds to the workstation on port 57555 over plain gRPC, leaving the device through the VRF that reaches the workstation. Prove the device connects out.

Reference. Subscription ID 101. Stream yang-push. Filter path /memory-ios-xe-oper:memory-statistics/memory-statistic. Encoding encode-kvgpb. Protocol grpc-tcp.

Verify.

  • Subscription listed as Configured and Valid.
  • Receiver state moves to a connected state (read the receiver view; ⚠ exact wording on 17.16).
  • On the workstation, a listener on 57555 (or a capture) shows a connection from the device's source address.
Hint

Thirty seconds in centiseconds is 3000. If the subscription is Valid but the workstation sees no SYN, look at the VRF and source-address before touching the filter.

Solution 
telemetry ietf subscription 101
 encoding encode-kvgpb
 filter xpath /memory-ios-xe-oper:memory-statistics/memory-statistic
 stream yang-push
 update-policy periodic 3000
 source-vrf <vrf that reaches workstation>   ! omit if default VRF
 receiver ip address <workstation-ip> 57555 protocol grpc-tcp
!
show telemetry ietf subscription 101 detail

With a bare nc -l listener the device will connect but the HTTP/2 conversation will not complete, so the subscription may not reach a healthy connected state. A capture proves the dial-out direction. A real gRPC collector is needed to see data (⚠ behavior unverified).

Recall check

Today

1. You change the receiver port on a live IOS XE subscription and the collector never sees the new port. Why, and what is the minimum correct sequence?

Receivers cannot be modified. Remove the subscription (or its receiver) with the no form and recreate it with the new receiver. Removal has to happen in configuration; a delete RPC does not remove configured subscriptions.

2. A colleague writes update-policy periodic 30 meaning thirty seconds. What actually happens?

The unit is centiseconds, so 30 is 0.3 s, below the documented minimum of 100 (one second). Use 3000 for 30 seconds. On NX-OS the same intent is sample-interval 30000, since that unit is milliseconds.

3. State two reasons dial-out suits a collector behind a firewall better than dial-in, and one thing the device needs in order to reach it.

The device opens the TCP session, so the firewall needs only an outbound rule from the device, and the collector needs no inbound path to the device. The device needs a route out of the right VRF and source address, set with source-vrf and source-address.

4. Subscription shows Valid. List the next three checks before you call it working.

Receiver connection state in the subscription detail or receiver view; reachability and port from the correct VRF; and evidence at the collector, by capture or decoded messages. Valid only says the configuration parsed.

From history

5. (from 2026-10-01) Why does a NETCONF dial-in subscription disappear after a switchover, and where would you see (or not see) it in the router config?

It is a dynamic subscription tied to the client session, so it is not in the running configuration and does not survive. Contrast with today's configured subscriptions, which are in config and reconnect.

6. (from 2026-09-28) Why does IOSvL2 accepting restconf into its configuration not mean RESTCONF actually works there?

The command parses, but the image has no RESTCONF stack behind it. Only Cat8000V gives you NETCONF and RESTCONF in this lab.
